Many UK businesses ask the same question: are daily van checks a legal requirement, or just good practice?

The honest answer is more nuanced than a simple yes or no. It depends on how the van is used, whether the business holds an Operator Licence, and what duties the employer has for workplace vehicles. What is clear is that DVSA, GOV.UK and HSE guidance all push strongly towards a meaningful daily walkaround check — and DVSA’s focus on light goods vehicles has increased.

This guide explains what the official sources say, what you should check, whether you need to record it, and why an MOT certificate is not a substitute for a daily check.

## Are daily van checks legally required?

There is no single line in UK law that says every private van must complete a signed digital form every morning. That is not the right way to frame the question.

The practical position for most businesses is:

- **Private leisure use** — you remain responsible for keeping the vehicle roadworthy, but formal fleet-style daily recording is usually lighter.
- **Company vans used for work** — employers have legal duties around workplace transport and work equipment. Daily checks are strongly expected as part of a safe system of work.
- **Commercial goods or passenger-carrying operations** — DVSA’s Guide to Maintaining Roadworthiness expects a meaningful daily walkaround check.
- **Operator Licence holders** — obligations are more formal: walkaround checks, defect recording, prompt repair, and retention of maintenance records.

So if you run vans for work, treating daily checks as optional is a poor risk decision — even where your exact legal paperwork differs from a heavy goods operator.

## What does DVSA say about daily van checks?

GOV.UK publishes dedicated guidance for van drivers: [Carry out van daily walkaround checks](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/carry-out-van-daily-walkaround-checks).

That guidance says drivers should carry out a walkaround check before their journey to make sure the van is safe. Where a business has an Operator Licence, defects found during the daily check or during the journey should be recorded and reported.

DVSA’s [Guide to Maintaining Roadworthiness](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guide-to-maintaining-roadworthiness/guide-to-maintaining-roadworthiness-commercial-goods-and-passenger-carrying-vehicles) (updated April 2025) goes further. It states that a driver or responsible person must undertake a **meaningful daily walkaround check**, and that defects which make the vehicle unroadworthy must be rectified before the vehicle is used.

That “meaningful daily walkaround check” phrase is useful. It means a real inspection of the vehicle’s condition — not a rushed tick-box after the van has already left the yard.

## What should you check on a van every day?

Official van walkaround guidance covers the main safety areas drivers should look at before setting off. Typical items include:

- Lights and indicators
- Tyres and wheels
- Brakes
- Steering
- Mirrors, cameras and glass
- Windscreen and wipers
- Warning lights on the dash
- Fluid levels and obvious leaks
- Battery condition where relevant
- Bodywork and doors
- Load security
- Exhaust
- Number plate
- Tow bars and trailers where fitted
- Specialist equipment used for the job

Your company policy may add items for racking, signage, first-aid kits, fire extinguishers or blue-light / emergency equipment. The point is consistency: every driver should know what “checked” means before they use the vehicle.

## Do you have to record daily van checks?

Carrying out a check and being able to prove it are related, but not identical.

Operator Licence holders are expected to keep safety inspection and maintenance records for **at least 15 months**, and to ensure drivers perform a walkaround check before driving for the first time each day, record defects, repair promptly, and take unroadworthy vehicles out of service. See [Being a goods vehicle operator: Maintaining your vehicles](https://www.gov.uk/being-a-goods-vehicle-operator/maintaining-your-vehicles).

Even outside formal Operator Licence regimes, recording checks is increasingly important because:

- DVSA can ask about walkaround processes at the roadside
- Insurance claims, client audits and employment disputes often turn on evidence
- Nil-defect (“all clear”) records show the system was followed when nothing was wrong

If your process only keeps paperwork when something breaks, you have a gap. We cover that separately in [nil-defect reports: why “no faults found” still needs a record](https://www.fleettrackpro.co.uk/compliance-centre/nil-defect-reports-why-they-matter/).

## What happens if a defect is found?

A workable process looks like this:

**Check → Identify defect → Report → Assess → Repair → Recheck → Return to service**

If a fault makes the van unsafe, it should not stay in use. Managers need visibility quickly, and fitters or workshops need a clear job status until the vehicle is ready again.

That full loop is explained in [closing defects before return to service](https://www.fleettrackpro.co.uk/compliance-centre/closing-defects-return-to-service/).

## Does an MOT mean my van is safe?

No.

GOV.UK is clear that a vehicle can still be unsafe even with a [current MOT certificate](https://www.gov.uk/check-vehicle-safe). An MOT is a periodic statutory test. It does not prove the van was roadworthy this morning, after yesterday’s site work, or with today’s load.

Think of three separate layers:

- **MOT / tax** — periodic legal status and reminders
- **Daily walkaround check** — ongoing roadworthiness before use
- **Maintenance and defect close-out** — keeping the vehicle safe between checks

Missing any one of those layers creates risk.

## What are employers legally responsible for?

HSE guidance for workplace transport and work vehicles is highly relevant for ordinary van-based businesses, not only Operator Licence fleets.

HSE material on [inspection, maintenance and repair](https://www.hse.gov.uk/workplacetransport/vehicles/inspection.htm) notes that drivers should check items such as tyres, lights and indicators at the start of every shift, and that employers may find it helpful to give drivers a list of daily checks to sign off.

Employer road safety guidance also recommends that companies:

- Carry out and record daily vehicle checks
- Have procedures for reporting defects
- Remedy defects promptly
- Prevent unsafe vehicles from being driven
- Carry out planned preventative maintenance
- Make sure privately owned vehicles used for work are safe

See [HSE: Make sure drivers and riders are safe](https://www.hse.gov.uk/roadsafety/employer/maintain-vehicles.htm).

For company drivers, that is why pre-use checks sit inside health and safety duties as well as transport compliance. More detail is in [pre-use vehicle checks for company drivers](https://www.fleettrackpro.co.uk/compliance-centre/pre-use-checks-company-vehicles/).

## Why DVSA is paying more attention to vans

In August 2025, DVSA published its Light Goods Vehicle strategy. It said it checks approximately **20,000 LGVs each year**, and that **more than half of those encounters result in enforcement action** for serious defects, insecure loads or significant overloads.

That does not mean half of all UK vans are defective. Enforcement is often targeted. It does mean that when vans are stopped, roadworthiness and load issues are common findings — and DVSA has said it will focus more on higher-risk LGV sectors.

[Read DVSA’s LGV strategy explanation](https://movingon.blog.gov.uk/2025/08/04/what-our-new-light-goods-vehicle-strategy-means-for-you/).

For related enforcement context on weight, see [van overloading: what new DVSA data means for fleets](https://www.fleettrackpro.co.uk/compliance-centre/van-overloading-dvsa-enforcement-data/).

## What happens during a roadside DVSA check?

DVSA can stop commercial vans and inspect roadworthiness. Outcomes can include advice, prohibitions and other enforcement action if the vehicle is unsafe. GOV.UK covers roadside checks for commercial drivers here: [Roadside vehicle checks](https://www.gov.uk/roadside-vehicle-checks-for-commercial-drivers).

Officers may ask about the walkaround process and recent defect history. Having today’s check, named driver attribution, photos where relevant, and a clear defect trail makes that conversation much easier. See [preparing for a DVSA roadside check](https://www.fleettrackpro.co.uk/compliance-centre/preparing-for-dvsa-roadside-check/).

## Bottom line for 2026

Avoid claiming that “the UK introduced a brand-new law in 2026 making daily van checks mandatory for everyone.” That overstates the position.

The accurate, commercially useful message is:

**Daily vehicle checks are already an established roadworthiness expectation for commercial and work vehicles. DVSA’s Guide to Maintaining Roadworthiness calls for a meaningful daily walkaround check, HSE expects employers to control vehicle risks, Operator Licence holders have formal recording duties, and DVSA’s increased focus on light goods vehicles means van fleets should treat inspection and defect reporting seriously.**

If you are still relying on memory, WhatsApp threads or incomplete paper packs, 2026 is a good year to tighten the process.

## Key takeaways

- Daily van checks are strongly expected for work and commercial use, even where paperwork rules differ by Operator Licence status
- DVSA guidance calls for a meaningful daily walkaround check and prompt repair of unroadworthy defects
- Employers have HSE duties around inspection, defect reporting and preventing unsafe vehicles being driven
- An MOT does not prove the van is safe today
- Recording checks — including nil-defect results — strengthens roadside and audit evidence
- Digital checks make timestamps, photos and defect close-out easier to manage than paper alone

This article summarises general principles and is not legal advice. Always check current GOV.UK and HSE guidance for your operation.
